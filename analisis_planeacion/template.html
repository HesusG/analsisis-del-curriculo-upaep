Context Engineering

From prompt engineering to the discipline that defines modern AI development

01
July – November 2023

The Foundations

Liu et al. (Stanford) · Greg Kamradt
Two landmark studies revealed that LLMs don't treat their context windows uniformly — attention has a shape, and that shape has consequences.

In July 2023, researchers at Stanford published "Lost in the Middle", demonstrating a striking U-shaped attention pattern. Language models paid close attention to the beginning and end of their context windows but significantly degraded on information placed in the middle. This wasn't a quirk of one model — it was a structural property of transformer attention.

Months later, in November 2023, Greg Kamradt released the "Needle in a Haystack" benchmark — a deceptively simple test that asked models to find a planted fact within a sea of filler text. Every frontier model aced it. But as Kelly Hong of Chroma later pointed out, the benchmark was essentially a lexical matching exercise requiring zero reasoning.

Kelly Hong's analysis cuts to the heart of the problem: "The question is 'What was the best writing advice I got from my college classmate?' and the needle is literally 'The best writing advice I got from my college classmate was to write every week.' The model just needs to perform a simple lexical match, not reason through ambiguity or do any deeper processing."

This created a dangerous disconnect. Labs showcased perfect NIAH scores in their marketing, implying that their full context windows were equally reliable for any task. The reality was far more nuanced — and it would take nearly two years for the field to fully reckon with this gap.

Takeaway

Context position matters. Not all tokens in the window receive equal attention, and passing a simple identification task tells you nothing about real-world performance.

02
February 2024

Compound AI Systems

Matei Zaharia · Berkeley BAIR · Drew Breunig
The shift from monolithic model calls to multi-component systems — where retrieval, tools, memory, and coordination work together — redefined what it meant to "build with AI."

In early 2024, Matei Zaharia and the Berkeley BAIR lab published their position paper on Compound AI Systems. The central argument: the most effective AI applications aren't single model calls — they're engineered systems with multiple interacting components. Retrievers, tool callers, memory stores, evaluators, and orchestrators all collaborating around a central LLM.

Drew Breunig observed this pattern emerging across real companies: "The data pipeline people have been doing this quietly at their own conferences. The agents and application builders are only just now starting to think about it." He pointed to teams like Cognition who discovered that their large coding agent spent 50% of its time on code search — so they trained a specialized model just for that subtask, cutting latency by 40% with identical quality.

Drew Breunig identified two categories of AI builders: data pipeline engineers who dealt with scale and reliability from day one, and application builders who were "still held together with vibe, duct tape, and system prompts." The compound AI insight was that the former group's techniques — decomposition, specialized models, multi-step pipelines — were becoming essential for everyone.

As he put it: "In the data world, you're at scale from day zero. If I'm writing a pipeline to process a terabyte of PDFs, I can't throw that at Opus 4.5. I'd be spending a ton of money or waiting a month."

Takeaway

The future belongs to systems, not models. The best AI applications compose multiple specialized components rather than relying on a single monolithic model call.

03
March – November 2024

The Long Context Promise

Google DeepMind · Anthropic · Drew Breunig
Context windows grew from 32K to 1M tokens in a single year. But bigger windows didn't automatically mean better understanding — they created a dangerous illusion of capability.

2024 was the year of the long context arms race. Google's Gemini 1.5 launched in March with a 1M token context window. Anthropic released Contextual Retrieval in September, followed by the Model Context Protocol (MCP) in November. The narrative was intoxicating: RAG would die, you'd just throw everything into the window, and the model would figure it out.

But Drew Breunig discovered a telling detail buried in the Gemini 2.5 technical paper's appendix: "Anytime they went over 200,000 tokens, it all kind of went south." A throwaway line that contradicted the marketing of the rest of the paper. This tension — between what labs promised and what engineers experienced — would define the next chapter of the field.

Drew Breunig described the moment: "One of my favorite technical papers was Gemini 2.5. In the appendix, they did two or three pages explaining how they got Gemini to play Pokemon. They actually walked through the harness, the prompts — things labs never share. And in there was one throwaway line: anytime they went over 200,000 tokens, it all kind of went south."

He compared this to the 2024 conversation: "Long context would come and save us all. We didn't need harnesses. We didn't need RAG. You could just throw the entire internet into the context and the problem would be solved. Here we had a very different message."

Takeaway

Bigger context windows are a capability, not a solution. The gap between theoretical capacity and practical reliability created the need for a new engineering discipline.

04
December 2024

Building Effective Agents

Anthropic · Hammad Bashir (Chroma CTO)
Anthropic's guide established a critical distinction: workflows are human-defined, agents are model-driven. Both require fundamentally different approaches to context management.

In December 2024, Anthropic published "Building Effective Agents" — a guide that cut through the hype with a simple principle: start with the simplest solution that could work, then add complexity only when needed. The key insight was the distinction between workflows (human-defined multi-step processes) and agents (model-driven autonomous systems).

Hammad Bashir of Chroma framed the landscape through three LLM topologies: the single function call, the composed workflow, and the agentic loop. "LLMs are just another unreliable non-deterministic component in your software stack," he argued. "They're just a function. You call them, they take some input and produce some output. We don't need to make them more complicated than that."

Hammad proposed a powerful framing: "If we want to make AI systems economically productive, what you're really maximizing is the lowest number of interventions per token. The number of human interventions per mile is the agentic metric for self-driving cars. We can think about an agentic metric for AI systems."

He demonstrated the fragility with a deceptively simple email classification task: given four natural-language rules about email priority, models would fail under trivial perturbations like removing spacing or changing a few words. "If you just take this prompt and remove the spacing, the output will change."

Takeaway

Agents aren't magic — they're composed software systems. Reliability comes from engineering discipline, not model capability.

Building Effective Agents — Anthropic
05
April 2025

12 Factor Agents

Dex Horthy (HumanLayer)
A manifesto that reframed LLMs as pure functions and established the "own your context window" principle — the first operational playbook for context engineering.

On April 22, 2025, Dex Horthy published "12 Factor Agents" — a manifesto inspired by the 12-Factor App principles. The core insight: "LLMs are pure functions. The only thing that improves the quality of your outputs is the quality of what you put in, which is your context window."

Horthy's team discovered through practice that context utilization past ~40% led to degraded performance — a "dumb zone" where models became unreliable. Their solution was a three-phase workflow: Research → Plan → Implement, with intentional context compaction between each phase. "If I'm shouting at Claude, it's because the plan was bad."

"A bad line of code is a bad line of code. A bad part of a plan can be hundreds of bad lines of code. And a bad line of research — a misunderstanding of how the system works — can be thousands of bad lines of code."

This hierarchy inverted where developers spent their time. Instead of reviewing code line-by-line, Horthy's team reviewed research files and implementation plans. "I haven't opened a non-markdown file in an editor in almost two months," he reported. Their intern shipped two PRs on his first day. By day eight, he shipped ten in a single day.

Takeaway

Own your context window. Keep utilization under 40%, invest in research and planning over code, and build your entire development workflow around context management.

June 18–27, 2025

The Critical Week

Karpathy · Lutke · Breunig · Martin · Willison
In ten days, "context engineering" went from niche term to industry consensus. A cascade of voices — from YC talks to viral tweets to taxonomies — crystallized a discipline.

What happened in late June 2025 was less a single event than a chain reaction. Andrej Karpathy's YC talk provided the metaphor. Tobi Lutke's tweet provided the signal. Drew Breunig's framework provided the structure. And the community provided the amplification. In ten days, a term that had been circulating among practitioners became the central framing for an entire discipline.

Jun 18 — Karpathy's YC Talk

Andrej Karpathy presents at YC, introducing the metaphor that would define the discourse: "The LLM is the CPU, context is the RAM." Just as programs need their data loaded into RAM, AI systems need their context engineered into the window. The term "context engineering" is foregrounded as the successor to "prompt engineering."

Jun 19 — Lutke's Canonical Tweet

Shopify CEO Tobi Lutke tweets: "I don't think 'prompt engineering' is going to be a thing... the real skill is context engineering." The tweet goes viral — 1.9 million views. It signals that the reframing isn't just technical; it's strategic.

Jun 22 — Breunig's "How Long Contexts Fail"

Drew Breunig publishes his synthesis of how long contexts fail, identifying four failure modes: attention degradation, distractor sensitivity, ambiguity compounding, and reasoning collapse. The post had been published earlier to modest reception — Karpathy's framing made it explode.

Jun 23 — Martin's Taxonomy

Lance Martin of LangChain publishes a taxonomy of context engineering operations: Write, Select, Compress, Isolate. This gives practitioners a shared vocabulary for the different kinds of work involved in curating context windows.

Jun 25 — Karpathy's Viral "+1"

Karpathy tweets his endorsement: "I think 'context engineering' is a much better term." The "+1" from the field's most respected educator amplifies everything that came before.

Jun 27 — Willison's Endorsement

Simon Willison publishes a blog post acknowledging context engineering as "a better term" and pointing to Breunig's work as the substantive framework. The endorsement from Willison — trusted by a vast developer audience — seals the consensus.

"I published the first article on how long contexts fail and kind of nothing happened. I was lucky enough to be on a bicycling vacation in Napa with my wife. I was a little disappointed. This is something I very rarely put long research efforts into writing."

"Then Karpathy said 'context engineering.' Suddenly everybody scrambled to find context engineering pieces. Simon Willison and someone else pointed to my pieces and they're like 'this is the only framework we have.'"

He later reflected on a conversation with Stanford professor Chris Potts: "'Sometimes it's hard to convince people because they're just so excited about AI,' and he got that thousand-yard stare and said, 'Or you can just wait for Karpathy to say it in a couple months.'"

Takeaway

Disciplines don't emerge from a single paper — they crystallize when the right voices, the right frameworks, and the right frustrations converge in a short window of time.

07
July 2025

Context Rot

Jeff Huber · Kelly Hong (Chroma)
Chroma's technical report proved what practitioners had felt: model performance doesn't just plateau with more tokens — it actively degrades. Context rot gave the problem a name and the field its urgency.

Chroma's research team, led by Kelly Hong, published a technical report testing 18 frontier models on tasks of increasing input length. The findings were stark: even on a task as simple as replicating a list of words, performance dropped precipitously past a few thousand tokens. Models would hallucinate extra words, skip entries, or generate random outputs entirely.

Jeff Huber framed the implication with characteristic directness: "Context rot implies the need for context engineering." He introduced the Gather-and-Glean framework — first maximize recall (gather all possibly relevant information), then maximize precision (remove everything irrelevant). "Brute forcing search" with LLMs as rerankers emerged as a dominant pattern.

1. Models struggle with reasoning over long conversations. On the LongMemEval benchmark (~120K tokens of chat history), all models performed dramatically better on condensed (~300 token) versions versus full context.

2. Ambiguity compounds the challenge of long inputs. At short lengths, models handle even highly ambiguous needle-question pairs. But as input grows, performance on ambiguous cases collapses first.

3. Distractors become devastating at scale. Topically related but incorrect information — common in any real knowledge base — causes models to confuse answers at long contexts even when they distinguish them perfectly at short lengths.

4. Models cannot be treated as reliable computing systems. Even on pure replication tasks (repeat this list exactly), models fail past 500 words. "This serves as another demonstration that models do not process their context uniformly."

Takeaway

Your model's context window is a theoretical maximum, not an operational budget. Engineer your context: maximize relevant information, minimize irrelevant context.

08
Late 2025

The Discipline Matures

Philipp Schmid · Anthropic · Gartner · O'Reilly
Context engineering transitions from practitioner insight to recognized discipline — with academic surveys, industry taxonomies, and formal institutional recognition.

By late 2025, the field had accumulated enough mass to attract institutional attention. An academic survey cataloged over 1,400 papers touching on aspects of context engineering. Gartner added context engineering to their emerging technology radar. Anthropic published a formal treatment of context engineering principles. O'Reilly published Drew Breunig's work.

Jeff Huber reflected on the arc: "Memory is the benefit; context engineering is the tool that gives you that benefit." The field was no longer about whether context engineering mattered — it was about building the frameworks, tools, and practices to do it systematically. As Huber put it: "Context engineering implies the existence of context engineers... and even a context engine."

The maturation followed a familiar pattern in software history. First came the practitioners discovering patterns through experience. Then the frameworks that codified those patterns. Then the benchmarks that measured adherence. And finally, the institutional recognition that signaled the field's permanence.

Lance Martin's taxonomy (Write, Select, Compress, Isolate) provided the operational vocabulary. Chroma's context rot report provided the empirical foundation. Dex Horthy's 12 Factor Agents provided the operational playbook. And Karpathy's CPU/RAM metaphor provided the conceptual frame that made it all click for a mass audience.

Takeaway

Context engineering has graduated from folk knowledge to formal discipline. The question is no longer "what is it?" but "how do we do it well?"

The Six Principles of Context Engineering

Distilled from 8 moments, 14 transcripts, and dozens of research papers

01

Less Is More

Precision over volume. Models degrade with more tokens, not less. Every irrelevant token is noise that displaces signal.

Liu et al. · Kamradt · Breunig
02

Gather, Then Glean

First maximize recall — cast a wide net. Then maximize precision — ruthlessly cut everything that doesn't serve the current turn.

Jeff Huber · Chroma
03

Own Your Context Window

Never let frameworks, defaults, or conversation history decide what's in your context. You are the architect of every token.

Dex Horthy · HumanLayer
04

Engineer, Don't Prompt

Context engineering is systems design, not wordsmithing. Build pipelines, harnesses, and retrieval systems — not clever strings.

Karpathy · Lutke
05

Context Is Memory

The context window is volatile RAM, not a static prompt. Memory is the benefit; context engineering is the tool that delivers it.

Karpathy · Jeff Huber
06

Test Your Context

Evals, not vibes. Build golden datasets, measure retrieval quality, and treat context curation as a testable engineering practice.

Breunig · Martin · Hammad Bashir
